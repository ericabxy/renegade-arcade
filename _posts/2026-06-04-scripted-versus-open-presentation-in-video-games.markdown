Historically speaking, video game level design can sometimes be divided into two categories, namely "scripted" and "open". Although levels must always be meticulously designed for player experience, these categories represent nearly opposite approaches.

Scripted levels go to an extreme of scripted progression through the level geometry, timed arrival of non-player characters, timed appearence of object, etc.. Shooters like _Gradius_ and _Raiden_ move the player forward through a level at a fixed pace while enemies and objects arrive at strictly timed points. Platformers like _Super Mario Bros._ and _Turrican_ move at the player's discretion, but still have a set beginning and end to each level and even a timer to limit time spent in the level. All of them progress through one level at a time and move to another. Branching paths like SMB "Warp Zones" and _Star Fox 64_ win conditions introduce some player choice and variety, but keep the relentless forward-pace intact.

Open levels are much less scripted, instead responding to player choices without urging them toward a particular pace or direction. These games are characterized by exploration, returning to previous areas with new goals, figuring out how to make progress, etc.. Arrival of non-player characters are "timed" to occur when the player approaches them. Objects are placed for the player to discover them. Level geometry moves by at the player's pace and can even temporarily impede the player until they decide how to progress. Adventure games like _The Legend of Zelda_ and _Metroid_ take advantage of this pace to present the world non-linearly, and branching paths can lead to whole different areas, optional content, risk-reward decisions, and more.

In my experience with game design I find scripted levels to take a lot more investment. Not only do you have to design a physical engine for interaction with the game world, but you have to compose a feature-length amount of scripted content to put the player through. It requires the amount of work of at least two people, a designer and an engineer, and a lot of complicated back-and-forth between the second-to-second _feel_ of play and the overall _presentation_ of content.
